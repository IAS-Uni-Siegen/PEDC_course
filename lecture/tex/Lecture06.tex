\begin{frame}{Why Do We Need Wide-Bandgap Devices?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Conventional Si devices have enabled modern power electronics, but they face practical limits at high voltage, high temperature, and high switching frequency.
    \item In many converters, the device is no longer the only limitation; passive components, cooling system, EMI filters, and packaging dominate size, weight, and cost.
    \item Wide-bandgap (WBG) devices allow faster switching and lower losses, enabling compact and efficient converters.
    \item The major motivation is not simply to replace Si devices, but to redesign the whole converter around higher frequency, higher temperature, and higher power density.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Do We Need Wide-Bandgap Devices?}

\begin{block}{Typical Motivating Examples}
\begin{itemize}
    \item EV traction inverters and on-board chargers
    \item Fast chargers and high-power DC--DC converters
    \item Low-inductance and high-speed motor drives
    \item Aerospace and integrated motor drives
    \item Renewable-energy converters and PV inverters
    \item Data-center and high-frequency power supplies
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Key Design Promise}
Lower loss + higher switching frequency + higher temperature capability
$\Rightarrow$ smaller passives, smaller cooling system, and higher power density.
\end{block}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Are They Called ``Wide-Bandgap'' Devices?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The bandgap $E_g$ is the energy difference between the valence band and the conduction band of a semiconductor.
    \item Silicon has a bandgap of about $1.1$ eV, whereas SiC and GaN have bandgaps above $3$ eV.
    \item A larger bandgap usually means lower intrinsic carrier concentration, lower leakage current, and better high-temperature blocking capability.
    \item More importantly for power devices, WBG materials can sustain a much higher critical electric field before breakdown.
    \item This allows a thinner and more highly doped drift region, reducing specific on-resistance for a given blocking voltage.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Crystal Structure of Silicon Carbide}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Silicon carbide (SiC) is a compound semiconductor made from silicon and carbon atoms in an approximately 1:1 ratio.
    \item Each Si atom forms covalent bonds with four neighbouring C atoms, and each C atom bonds with four neighbouring Si atoms.
    \item The local bonding is approximately tetrahedral and is associated with $sp^3$ hybridization.
    \item The strong Si--C bond is one of the physical reasons for SiC having a large bandgap, high breakdown field, and high-temperature capability.
    \item Unlike Si, SiC does not have only one common crystal form; it appears in several polytypes due to different stacking sequences.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{SiC Polytypes: 3C, 4H, and 6H}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC crystals are formed by stacking Si--C bilayers in different sequences.
    \item The notation \textbf{C}, \textbf{H}, and \textbf{R} denotes cubic, hexagonal, and rhombohedral crystal systems.
    \item Common SiC polytypes include \textbf{3C-SiC}, \textbf{4H-SiC}, and \textbf{6H-SiC}.
    \item The number indicates the number of Si--C double atomic layers in one repeating unit along the stacking direction.
    \item For power devices, \textbf{4H-SiC} is preferred because it provides a favourable combination of bandgap, carrier transport, breakdown field, and mature wafer technology.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Breakdown Field and Intrinsic Carrier Concentration}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The breakdown electric field is the field strength at which the semiconductor can no longer block voltage without avalanche multiplication.
    \item SiC has a breakdown field of approximately $2.5~\mathrm{MV/cm}$, about ten times higher than Si.
    \item Therefore, for the same blocking voltage, the SiC drift region can be thinner and more heavily doped than in a Si device.
    \item This reduces the drift-region resistance, which is usually the dominant part of $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ in high-voltage MOSFETs.
    \item SiC also has a much lower intrinsic carrier concentration than Si:
    \[
        n_i(\mathrm{SiC}) \ll n_i(\mathrm{Si})
    \]
    \item The lower $n_i$ allows SiC devices to retain blocking capability at much higher junction temperatures.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Commercial SiC MOSFET Device Structures}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Commercial SiC MOSFETs use vertical current flow so that high current and high blocking voltage can be obtained within a practical chip area.
    \item The lightly doped $n^-$ drift region supports the blocking voltage, while the channel and source/body regions control the conduction path.
    \item Planar SiC MOSFETs were the first commercial technology, but trench structures are widely used to reduce channel resistance and increase cell density.
    \item In trench MOSFETs, electric-field crowding at the trench bottom is a critical reliability concern for the gate oxide.
    \item Shielded or double-trench structures reduce the electric field at critical oxide corners and improve reliability, switching performance, and power density.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
