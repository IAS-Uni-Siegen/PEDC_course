\begin{frame}{Why Do We Need Wide-Bandgap Devices?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Conventional Si devices have enabled modern power electronics, but they face practical limits at high voltage, high temperature, and high switching frequency.
    \item In many converters, the device is no longer the only limitation; passive components, cooling system, EMI filters, and packaging dominate size, weight, and cost.
    \item Wide-bandgap (WBG) devices allow faster switching and lower losses, enabling compact and efficient converters.
    \item The major motivation is not simply to replace Si devices, but to redesign the whole converter around higher frequency, higher temperature, and higher power density.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Do We Need Wide-Bandgap Devices?}

\begin{block}{Typical Motivating Examples}
\begin{itemize}
    \item EV traction inverters and on-board chargers
    \item Fast chargers and high-power DC--DC converters
    \item Low-inductance and high-speed motor drives
    \item Aerospace and integrated motor drives
    \item Renewable-energy converters and PV inverters
    \item Data-center and high-frequency power supplies
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Key Design Promise}
Lower loss + higher switching frequency + higher temperature capability
$\Rightarrow$ smaller passives, smaller cooling system, and higher power density.
\end{block}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Are They Called ``Wide-Bandgap'' Devices?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The bandgap $E_g$ is the energy difference between the valence band and the conduction band of a semiconductor.
    \item Silicon has a bandgap of about $1.1$ eV, whereas SiC and GaN have bandgaps above $3$ eV.
    \item A larger bandgap usually means lower intrinsic carrier concentration, lower leakage current, and better high-temperature blocking capability.
    \item More importantly for power devices, WBG materials can sustain a much higher critical electric field before breakdown.
    \item This allows a thinner and more highly doped drift region, reducing specific on-resistance for a given blocking voltage.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Crystal Structure of Silicon Carbide}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Silicon carbide (SiC) is a compound semiconductor made from silicon and carbon atoms in an approximately 1:1 ratio.
    \item Each Si atom forms covalent bonds with four neighbouring C atoms, and each C atom bonds with four neighbouring Si atoms.
    \item The local bonding is approximately tetrahedral and is associated with $sp^3$ hybridization.
    \item The strong Si--C bond is one of the physical reasons for SiC having a large bandgap, high breakdown field, and high-temperature capability.
    \item Unlike Si, SiC does not have only one common crystal form; it appears in several polytypes due to different stacking sequences.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{SiC Polytypes: 3C, 4H, and 6H}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC crystals are formed by stacking Si--C bilayers in different sequences.
    \item The notation \textbf{C}, \textbf{H}, and \textbf{R} denotes cubic, hexagonal, and rhombohedral crystal systems.
    \item Common SiC polytypes include \textbf{3C-SiC}, \textbf{4H-SiC}, and \textbf{6H-SiC}.
    \item The number indicates the number of Si--C double atomic layers in one repeating unit along the stacking direction.
    \item For power devices, \textbf{4H-SiC} is preferred because it provides a favourable combination of bandgap, carrier transport, breakdown field, and mature wafer technology.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Breakdown Field and Intrinsic Carrier \\Concentration of SiC}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC has a breakdown field of approximately $2.5~\mathrm{MV/cm}$, about ten times higher than Si.
    \item Therefore, for the same blocking voltage, the SiC drift region can be thinner and more heavily doped than in a Si device.
    \item This reduces the drift-region resistance, which is usually the dominant part of $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ in high-voltage MOSFETs.
    \item SiC also has a much lower intrinsic carrier concentration than Si: $n_i(\mathrm{SiC}) \ll n_i(\mathrm{Si})$.
    \item The lower $n_i$ allows SiC devices to retain blocking capability at much higher junction temperatures.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Commercial SiC MOSFET Device Structures}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Commercial SiC MOSFETs use vertical current flow so that high current and high blocking voltage can be obtained within a practical chip area.
    \item Lightly doped $n^-$ drift region supports blocking voltage, while the channel and source/body regions control the conduction path.
    \item Planar SiC MOSFETs were the first commercial technology, but trench structures are widely used to reduce channel resistance and increase cell density.
    \item In trench MOSFETs, electric-field crowding at the trench bottom is a critical reliability concern for the gate oxide.
    \item Shielded or double-trench structures reduce the electric field at critical oxide corners and improve reliability, switching performance, and power density.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Reverse Blocking Characteristics of SiC Diodes}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During reverse bias, the cathode is at a higher potential than the anode and only a small reverse leakage current flows.
    
    \item The reverse current $I_R$ depends on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reverse voltage $V_R$
        \item Junction temperature $T_j$
    \end{itemize}

    \item At low reverse voltage:$I_R \approx \text{pA to nA range}$ indicating effective blocking operation.

    \item As $V_R$ approaches the breakdown region, avalanche multiplication causes a rapid increase in current.

    \item SiC devices typically exhibit:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item High breakdown voltage margin
        \item Low leakage current
        \item Improved high-temperature blocking capability
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Forward Conduction Characteristics}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under forward bias, current flows from anode to cathode once the forward voltage exceeds the conduction threshold.
    
    \item The forward conduction behaviour is represented by the: $I_F - V_F $ characteristic.

    \item At low forward voltage: $I_F \approx 0$, diode remains OFF.

    \item Beyond the knee voltage, the current increases rapidly with applied voltage.

    \item The forward characteristics vary with junction temperature:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Forward voltage decreases with increasing $T_j$
        \item Temperature coefficient changes with operating current
    \end{itemize}

    \item The near-zero temperature coefficient region helps current sharing during parallel operation.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Junction Capacitance and Charge}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under reverse bias, a depletion region forms between the p-region and n-region.
    
    \item This depletion region behaves similarly to a voltage-dependent capacitor.

    \item The junction capacitance decreases with increasing reverse voltage: $C_j \downarrow \quad \text{as} \quad V_R \uparrow$.
    \item The stored junction charge is obtained from: $Q_C = \int_0^{V_R} C(V_R)\, dV_R$.
    \item Lower junction capacitance is beneficial because it:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reduces switching losses
        \item Improves high-frequency performance
        \item Reduces reverse recovery effects
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Junction Capacitance Energy and Practical Implications}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}

    \item The energy stored in junction capacitance is:
    \[
        E_C = \int_0^{V_R} C(V_R)\,V_R\,dV_R

    \begin{itemize}
        \item High-frequency switching
        \item Hard commutation
        \item Fast transient operation
    \end{itemize}

    \item Lower stored energy in SiC diodes contributes to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reduced switching loss
        \item Faster switching transitions
        \item Lower reverse recovery stress

\begin{frame}{Power Dissipation in SiC Power Diodes}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During operation, a SiC diode dissipates power mainly through forward conduction and switching-related capacitive effects.
    \item The junction temperature rises until thermal equilibrium is reached between generated heat and removed heat.
    \item With case temperature $T_C$ specified, the steady-state thermal relation is
    \[
        T_J - T_C = P_{\mathrm{tot}}(T_C)\,R_{\mathrm{th}(JC)}
    \]
    \item Therefore, the maximum allowable power dissipation decreases as $T_C$ increases.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Forward Current Limit and Surge Capability}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The allowable forward current is limited by the maximum dissipated power, duty cycle, and forward voltage drop.
    \item For duty cycle $D$, the current limit can be estimated as
    \[
        I_F =
        \frac{P_{\mathrm{tot}}(T_C)/D}
        {V_F\!\left(T_{J(\max)},I_F\right)}
    \]
    \item Datasheets therefore provide $I_F$--$T_C$ curves for different duty cycles.
    \item Surge forward current $I_{FSM}$ defines the maximum non-repetitive half-sinusoidal current the diode can withstand.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Forward Current Limit and Surge Capability}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The $i^2t$ rating measures short-time thermal withstand capability:
    \[
        i^2t=\int_0^{10\,\mathrm{ms}}
        \left(I_{FSM}\sin{\omega t}\right)^2dt
        \approx 0.005\,I_{FSM}^2
    \]
    \item These properties make SiC diodes highly suitable for:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item High-frequency converters
        \item EV traction systems
        \item PFC stages
        \item Renewable-energy converters
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\begin{block}{Design Meaning}
\small
At higher case or ambient temperature, less power can be dissipated before the junction reaches $T_{J(\max)}$.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Practical Point}
\small
Surge ratings are strongly dependent on pulse duration, repetition, and initial junction temperature; they should not be compared blindly across datasheets.
\end{block}

\end{column}
\end{columns}

\begin{frame}{SiC Power MOSFETs: Motivation and Challenges}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs enable:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item High blocking voltage
        \item Low specific ON-resistance
        \item High-temperature operation
        \item Fast switching capability
    \end{itemize}

    \item Baliga's figure of merit for 4H-SiC is more than 2000$\times$ higher than silicon.

    \item However, early commercialization faced major challenges:
    \begin{itemize}

        \item Low channel mobility
        \item High electric field stress in gate oxide
        \item Reliability concerns during blocking operation
    \end{itemize}

    \item These challenges motivated the development of advanced SiC MOSFET structures.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Reach-Through Limitation in SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item During blocking operation, the depletion region expands into the P-base region.

    \item If the P-base becomes fully depleted, reach-through breakdown occurs.

    \item Minimum P-base thickness:
    \[
    t_P=\frac{\varepsilon_s E_C}{qN_A}
    \]

    \item Due to the large critical electric field of SiC:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Higher P-base doping is required
        \item Threshold voltage increases significantly
    \end{itemize}

    \item This creates a trade-off between:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Blocking capability
        \item Threshold voltage
        \item Channel resistance
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage in SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The threshold voltage is the gate voltage required to form a strong inversion channel.

    \item For SiC planar MOSFETs:
    \[
    V_{TH}=
    \frac{\sqrt{4\varepsilon_skTN_A\ln(N_A/n_i)}}{C_{ox}}
    +\frac{2kT}{q}\ln\left(\frac{N_A}{n_i}\right)
    \]

    \item SiC devices typically exhibit:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Higher threshold voltage
        \item Lower channel mobility
    \end{itemize}

    \item Causes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Wide bandgap
        \item Very low intrinsic carrier concentration
        \item High P-base doping requirement
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Gate Oxide Electric Field in SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs experience very high electric field stress in the gate oxide during blocking operation.

    \item Oxide electric field:
    \[
    E_{ox}=
    \left(
    \frac{\varepsilon_{semi}}
    {\varepsilon_{oxide}}
    \right)
    E_{semi}

    \item For 4H-SiC:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Semiconductor electric field can reach very high values
        \item Oxide field may exceed SiO$_2$ reliability limits
    \end{itemize}

    \item This can cause:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Oxide degradation
        \item Hot electron injection
        \item Threshold voltage shift
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Accumulation-Mode SiC MOSFET}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The accumulation-mode structure uses an N-base region below the gate oxide.

    \[
    E_1=
    \frac{V_{bi}}{W_N}
    -
    \frac{qN_DW_N}{2\varepsilon_s}
    \]

    E_{ox}=
    \frac{\varepsilon_s}{\varepsilon_{ox}}E_1
    \]
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Leakage Current and Breakdown Voltage}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item With \(V_{GS}=0\), the SiC MOSFET blocks the applied drain voltage \(V_{DS}\).

    \item A small leakage current \(I_{DSS}\) flows in the OFF-state.

    \item \(I_{DSS}\) increases with:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Increasing \(V_{DS}\)
        \item Increasing junction temperature \(T_J\)
    \end{itemize}

    \item At sufficiently high \(V_{DS}\), avalanche breakdown occurs:
    \[
    V_{DS} \rightarrow V_{(BR)DSS}
    \]

    \item SiC MOSFETs usually exhibit breakdown voltages significantly above their nominal ratings.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Temperature Dependence of Breakdown Voltage}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.55\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The breakdown voltage increases with junction temperature:
    \[
    V_{(BR)DSS} \uparrow \quad \text{as} \quad T_J \uparrow
    \]

    \item Higher temperature reduces carrier mobility due to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Lattice scattering
        \item Impurity scattering
    \end{itemize}

    \item Reduced mobility makes avalanche multiplication more difficult.

    \item The breakdown voltage is commonly defined at a specified leakage current: $I_{(BR)DSS}$.

    \item Typical values of \(I_{(BR)DSS}\) range from:$ 100~\mu A \; \text{to} \; 5~mA$.

\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Power Dissipation and Drain Current}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Power losses increase the junction temperature of the MOSFET.

    \item The steady-state thermal relation is:
    \[
    T_J-T_C=P_{tot}(T_C)\cdot R_{th(JC)}
    \]

    \item As case temperature \(T_C\) increases:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Maximum allowable power decreases
        \item Maximum drain current decreases
    \end{itemize}

    \item Continuous drain current is approximated by:
    \[
    I_D=
    \sqrt{
    \frac{P_{tot}(T_C)}
    {R_{DS(on)}(T_{J(max)})}
    }
    \]
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage Variations and DIBL Effect}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The threshold voltage exhibits strong dependence on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Junction temperature

    \end{itemize}

    \item SiC MOSFETs show significant: $\text{Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL)}$

    \item DIBL: Increasing \(V_{DS}\) reduces: $V_{GS(th)}$

    \item Combined effects of high temperature and DIBL may lead to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Unintended turn-ON
        \item Shoot-through faults
        \item Increased switching losses
    \end{itemize}

    \item Careful gate driver and layout design are therefore essential.
\end{itemize}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage Hysteresis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs exhibit threshold voltage hysteresis during repeated switching operation.

    \item The primary cause is the high density of interface traps at the SiC/SiO\(_2\) interface.

    \item Gate bias stress changes the trapped charge distribution:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Negative gate bias increases positive oxide traps
        \item Positive gate bias traps electrons in the oxide
    \end{itemize}

    \item As a result:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(V_{GS(th)}\) shifts dynamically
        \item Switching characteristics become history dependent
        \item Measurement repeatability becomes challenging
    \end{itemize}

    \item Threshold voltage hysteresis strongly affects:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Short-circuit performance
        \item False turn-ON behavior
        \item Gate charge characteristics
        \item Reliability evaluation
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dynamic Characteristics of SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dynamic behavior of a SiC MOSFET is governed mainly by its parasitic capacitances between gate, drain, and source terminals.
    \item Unlike bipolar devices, there is no excess minority-carrier storage in the main MOSFET channel; therefore, the switching speed can be very high.
    \item The key capacitances are:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Gate-source capacitance: $C_{GS}$
        \item Gate-drain capacitance: $C_{GD}$
        \item Drain-source capacitance: $C_{DS}$
    \end{itemize}
    \item These capacitances are strongly nonlinear because the depletion region changes with $V_{DS}$.
    \item During switching, the measured voltage and current waveforms are therefore shaped by both the gate-drive circuit and the voltage-dependent capacitances.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Junction Capacitances of SiC MOSFET}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_{GS}$ consists mainly of the channel-gate oxide capacitance and the gate-source overlap capacitance.
    \item $C_{GD}$ is the feedback capacitance and is strongly related to the Miller effect.
    \item $C_{DS}$ is mainly associated with the drain-source pn-junction depletion region.
    \item Datasheets usually report:
    \begin{align*}
        C_{iss} &= C_{GS}+C_{GD} \\
        C_{oss} &= C_{DS}+C_{GD} \\
        C_{rss} &= C_{GD}
    \end{align*}
    \item $C_{rss}$ is especially important because it controls the drain-voltage transition during switching.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Voltage Dependence of Capacitances}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The capacitance values are not constant during operation.
    \item As $V_{DS}$ increases, depletion region expands and junction capacitances decrease.
    \item $C_{iss}$ changes relatively little because it is dominated by $C_{GS}$.
    \item $C_{oss}$ and $C_{rss}$ show a stronger reduction with increasing $V_{DS}$.
    \item At high $V_{DS}$, the depletion region changes slowly; therefore, the capacitances become relatively constant.
    \item In practical switching analysis, the nonlinear capacitance behavior must be considered carefully.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Inductive Load Switching Circuit}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFET switching is commonly evaluated using an inductive-load circuit.
    \item The circuit includes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item DC bus capacitor $C_{Bus}$
        \item Load inductance $L$
        \item Gate resistance $R_G$
        \item Power-loop inductance $L_{Loop}$
        \item Drive-loop inductance $L_{DRV}$
    \end{itemize}
    \item The upper device body diode or external diode conducts during freewheeling.
    \item During turn-ON, the lower MOSFET takes over current from the diode.
    \item This process creates reverse recovery, current overshoot, voltage ringing, and high $dv/dt$.
\end{itemize}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-ON Process: Initial Charging Stage}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Before turn-ON, the lower SiC MOSFET is OFF:
    \[
    I_{DS}=0,\qquad V_{DS}=V_{Bus}
    \]
    \item At $t_0$, the gate drive changes from $V_{DRV(off)}$ to $V_{DRV(on)}$.
    \item The gate current charges the input capacitance:
    \[
    I_G = I_{CGS}+I_{CGD}
    \]
    \item Since $V_{DS}$ is still almost constant, $C_{GS}$ and $C_{GD}$ are approximately constant in this interval.
    \item The gate voltage rises exponentially toward the applied drive voltage:
    \[
    V_{GS}(t)=V_{DRV(on)}
    -\left(V_{DRV(on)}-V_{DRV(off)}\right)
    e^{-\frac{t-t_0}{R_G C_{iss}}}
    \]
    \item When $V_{GS}$ reaches $V_{GS(th)}$, the MOSFET starts conducting and the turn-ON delay ends.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-ON Process: Current Rise and Reverse Recovery}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item After $V_{GS}>V_{GS(th)}$, channel current starts to increase.
    \item In the saturation region:
    \[
    I_{channel}=K\left(V_{GS}-V_{GS(th)}\right)^2
    \]
    \item Initially:$I_{DS}\approx I_{channel}$
    \item The increasing $I_{DS}$ generates voltage drop across loop inductance:
    \[
    \Delta V_{DS}=L_{Loop}\frac{dI_{DS}}{dt}
    \]
    \item When $I_{DS}$ exceeds the load current, the diode enters reverse recovery and produces current overshoot.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.44\textwidth}
\vspace{-0.25cm}

\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-ON Process: Miller Ramp and Voltage Fall}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During the voltage-fall interval, $V_{DS}$ decreases rapidly while $I_{DS}$ is already high.
    \item The drain-gate capacitance current becomes important:
    \[
    I_{CGD}=C_{GD}\frac{dV_{GD}}{dt}
    \]
    \item Since:
    \[
    V_{GD}=V_{GS}-V_{DS}
    \]
    and usually:
    \[
    \left|\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}\right| \gg \left|\frac{dV_{GS}}{dt}\right|
    \]
    the feedback current is approximately:
    \[
    I_{CGD}\approx C_{GD}\left|\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}\right|
    \]
    \item Unlike silicon MOSFETs, SiC MOSFETs may not show a perfectly flat Miller plateau.
    \item The gate voltage can continue rising slowly due to DIBL, low channel mobility, and nonlinear $C_{GD}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-OFF Process of SiC MOSFET}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During turn-OFF, the gate drive changes from $V_{DRV(on)}$ to $V_{DRV(off)}$.
    \item The input capacitance is discharged through the gate resistance.
    \item Initially, $V_{DS}$ and $I_{DS}$ do not change significantly.
    \item As $V_{GS}$ decreases, $R_{DS(on)}$ increases and $V_{DS}$ begins to rise.
    \item The rising $V_{DS}$ charges $C_{DS}$ and discharges $C_{GD}$.
    \item The measured current includes both channel current and capacitive current components.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Turn-OFF Current Components}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During turn-OFF, the measured drain current is not equal to the true channel current.
    \item The channel current can be written as:
    \[
    I_{channel}
    =
    I_{DS}
    -
    C_{GD}\left|\frac{dV_{GD}}{dt}\right|
    -
    C_{DS}\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}
    \]
    \item As $V_{DS}$ rises, $C_{GD}$ decreases, so the voltage rise may accelerate.
    \item The rapid reduction of $I_{DS}$ can generate voltage overshoot through:
    \[
    \Delta V=L_{Loop}\frac{dI_{DS}}{dt}
    \]
    \item Oscillations may appear in $V_{GS}$, $V_{DS}$, and $I_{DS}$ due to coupling through $C_{GD}$ and parasitic inductances.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Switching Energy of SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Switching energy is usually calculated from the overlap of drain current and drain-source voltage:
    \[
    E=\int v_{DS}(t)i_{DS}(t)\,dt
    \]
    \item However, measured $I_{DS}$ includes capacitive current associated with $C_{oss}$.
    \item Therefore, the channel current differs between turn-ON and turn-OFF:
    \[
    I_{channel}=I_{DS}+I_{Coss}
    \]
    \[
    I_{channel}=I_{DS}-I_{Coss}
    \]
    \item The output capacitance energy is:
    \[
    E_{oss}(V_{Bus})=
    \int_{0}^{V_{Bus}} C_{oss}(V)\cdot V_{DS}(V)\,dV
    \]
    \item Corrected switching energies can be expressed as:
    \[
    E_{on}=\int I_{DS}(t)V_{DS}(t)\,dt+E_{oss}(V_{Bus})
    \]
    \[
    E_{off}=\int I_{DS}(t)V_{DS}(t)\,dt+E_{oss}(V_{Bus})
    \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Gate Charge Characteristics}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gate charge $Q_G$ represents the charge required to drive the MOSFET gate.
    \item It is obtained by integrating the gate current:
    \[
    Q_G=\int I_G(t)\,dt
    \]
    \item A constant-current gate drive is often used for measurement.
    \item $V_{GS}$ waveform can be interpreted in 4 regions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Charging of $C_{iss}$
        \item Current rise
        \item Miller region
        \item Full enhancement
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

    \begin{itemize}
    \item The gate charge determines gate-driver power and switching speed.
    \end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Key Design Implications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs switch very fast, but this makes parasitic inductance and capacitance more critical.
    \item $C_{GD}$ strongly influences $dv/dt$, Miller coupling, and false turn-ON.
    \item $C_{oss}$ affects measured switching energy and must be considered in loss calculation.
    \item Reverse recovery of the body diode can still produce current overshoot and voltage ringing.
    \item Gate resistance provides a practical way to tune:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Switching speed
        \item Voltage overshoot
        \item Current overshoot
        \item EMI generation
    \end{itemize}
    \item Accurate switching-loss evaluation requires consistent definitions of integration limits.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Figure of Merit (FOM) of SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Device selection in power electronics is not based on a single parameter.
    \item Engineers must simultaneously consider:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Conduction loss
        \item Switching loss
        \item Gate-drive loss
        \item Capacitances
        \item Thermal capability
    \end{itemize}
    \item Figure of Merit (FOM) parameters provide simplified metrics for comparing devices.
    \item A good FOM attempts to capture the tradeoff between:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Low conduction loss ($R_{DS(on)}$)
        \item Fast switching capability
        \item Low stored charge and capacitance
    \end{itemize}
    \item Different FOMs are useful for different applications such as:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Hard switching converters
        \item Soft-switching converters
        \item High-frequency operation
        \item High-voltage applications
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Baliga High-Frequency Figure of Merit (BHFFOM)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item J. Baliga proposed one of the earliest MOSFET figures of merit for high-frequency operation.
    \item The total loss is represented as the sum of:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Conduction loss due to $R_{DS(on)}$
        \item Switching loss associated with charging and discharging $C_{iss}$
    \end{itemize}

    \vspace{0.2cm}

    \[
    P_{loss}
    =
    I_{rms}^2\frac{R_{DS(on),sp}}{A}
    +
    C_{iss,sp}\cdot A \cdot V_{DRV}^2 \cdot f_s
    \]

    \item Based on this, the Baliga High-Frequency Figure of Merit is:
    \[
    \text{BHFFOM}
    =
    \frac{1}{R_{DS(on),sp}\cdot C_{iss,sp}}
    \]

    \item Since practical datasheets usually provide:
    \[
    R_{DS(on)}=\frac{R_{DS(on),sp}}{A}
    \]
    \[
    C_{iss}=C_{iss,sp}\cdot A
    \]

    the practical form becomes:
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{FOM}=R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_G
    }
    \]

    \item Lower FOM values generally indicate better switching performance.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{NHFFOM and KFOM}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Output capacitance $C_{oss}$ also contributes significantly to switching loss.
    \item Il-Jung Kim therefore proposed the Next High-Frequency Figure of Merit (NHFFOM).

    \[
    \text{NHFFOM}
    =
    \frac{1}{R_{DS(on),sp}\cdot C_{oss,sp}}
    \]

    \item Since direct use of specific capacitance is difficult, equivalent $C_{oss}$ is introduced:

    \[
    \frac{1}{2}C_{oss,eq}V^2
    =
    \int_0^V C_{oss}(V_{DS})V_{DS}\,dV_{DS}
    \]

    \item J. W. Kolar proposed the more practical KFOM: $\text{KFOM}
    =
    \frac{1}{R_{DS(on)}\cdot C_{oss,eq}}$

    \item KFOM is widely used in MHz converters because:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $C_{oss}$ strongly influences hard-switching loss
        \item Output capacitance energy becomes critical at high frequency
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{HDFOM and Idealized Switching Waveforms}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Alex Huang divided MOSFET turn-ON into several switching intervals:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Delay interval $t_d$
        \item Current rise interval $t_r$
        \item Voltage fall interval $t_f$
        \item Overdrive interval $t_{ov}$
    \end{itemize}

    \item The dominant switching loss in high-voltage devices is often associated with $t_f$.
    
    \item Based on this observation:
    \[
    P_{loss}
    =
    I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}
    +
    V\cdot I_L\cdot (t_r+t_f)f_s
    \]

    \item The resulting High-Frequency Dynamic Figure of Merit (HDFOM) becomes:

\end{itemize}

\column{0.44\textwidth}
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{HDFOM}
    =
    \sqrt{R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}}
    }
    \]

\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{YFOM for Hard-Switching Applications}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.50\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Yucheng Ying proposed a more detailed switching-loss model.
    \item Both turn-ON and turn-OFF transitions are considered separately.
    \item The switching intervals are related to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Gate resistance $R_G$
        \item Miller plateau voltage $V_{plt}$
        \item Gate charge components
    \end{itemize}

    \item Important timing equations:
    \[
    t_{r(on)}
    =
    \frac{Q_{gs2}R_G}{V_{DRV}-V_{plt}}
    \]

    \[
    t_{f(on)}
    =
    \frac{Q_{gd}R_G}{V_{DRV}-V_{plt}}
    \]

\end{itemize}

\column{0.46\textwidth}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/YFOM_switching_process.png}
\caption{Idealized switching process for turn-ON and turn-OFF.}
\end{figure}

\end{columns}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The proposed YFOM becomes:
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{YFOM}
    =
    (Q_{gd}+K_{gs2}Q_{gs2})\cdot R_{DS(on)}
    }
    \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{FFOM for Soft-Switching Converters}
\begin{itemize}
    \item YFOM mainly targets hard-switching converters.
    \item Under Zero-Voltage Switching (ZVS), the turn-ON loss becomes negligible.
    \item Therefore, only these are dominant.:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Turn-OFF loss
        \item Conduction loss
        \item Gate-drive loss
    \end{itemize}


    \item The switching loss model becomes:
    \[
    P_{loss}
    =
    \frac{V_sI_s}{2}
    \frac{(Q_{gd}+Q_{gs2})R_G}{V_{plt}}
    f_s
    +
    I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}
    +
    Q_GV_{DRV}f_s
    \]

    \item Dianbo Fu proposed the following Figure of Merit for ZVS systems:
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{FFOM}
    =
    (Q_{gd}+K_{loss}Q_{gs2})R_{DS(on)}
    }
    \]

    \item FFOM is particularly useful for:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item LLC resonant converters
        \item Phase-shifted full bridge converters
        \item High-frequency soft-switching applications
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Calculation of Power Device Losses}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Device loss analysis is essential for:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Thermal design
        \item Efficiency optimization
        \item Device selection
        \item Converter reliability
    \end{itemize}

    \item In practical converters, losses include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Conduction losses
        \item Switching losses
        \item Reverse recovery losses
        \item Gate-drive losses
    \end{itemize}

    \item Loss calculations can be performed using:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Manufacturer datasheets
        \item Experimental double-pulse testing
        \item Electrothermal simulations
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\column{0.48\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Datasheet-based approaches are fast and convenient but are limited to specified operating conditions.
    
    \item Experimental measurements provide higher accuracy because:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Parasitics are included
        \item Real switching conditions are captured
        \item Reverse recovery behavior is measured directly
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Conduction Loss Calculation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Conduction loss is generated when the MOSFET carries current in the ON-state.
    \item Instantaneous conduction power is: $p(t)=v_{DS}(t)\cdot i_{DS}(t)$
    \item Average conduction loss over one switching period:
    \[
    P_C^{MOS}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \int_0^{T_s}
    V_{DS}(I_{DS}(t),T_J,V_{DRV(on)})
    \cdot
    I_{DS}(t)\,dt
    \]

    \item Since SiC MOSFET conduction is approximately resistive:
    \[
    P_C^{MOS}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \int_0^{T_s}
    R_{DS(on)}
    \cdot
    I_{DS}^2(t)\,dt
    \]

    \item Body-diode conduction loss is:
    \[
    P_C^{MOS,BD}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \int_0^{T_s}
    V_F(I_F,T_J)\cdot I_F(t)\,dt
    \]

    \item Third-quadrant conduction must include both channel conduction and body-diode conduction effects.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Switching and Reverse Recovery Losses}

\begin{columns}
\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Switching losses occur due to overlap between:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Drain-source voltage $V_{DS}$
        \item Drain current $I_{DS}$
    \end{itemize}

    \item Average turn-ON switching loss:
    \[
    P_{on}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \sum E_{on}
    \]

    \item Average turn-OFF switching loss:
    \[
    P_{off}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \sum E_{off}
    \]
    \end{itemize}
\column{0.52\textwidth}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item These energies are influenced by:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Junction temperature
        \item Gate resistance
        \item Loop inductance
        \item Switching current
        \item Bus voltage
    \end{itemize}

    \item Reverse recovery loss is:
    \[
    P_{rr}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \sum E_{rr}
    \]

    \item Reverse recovery strongly depends on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reverse current
        \item $di/dt$
        \item Junction temperature
        \item External circuit parasitics
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}  
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Total Loss Calculation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Total MOSFET loss is the sum of all individual loss mechanisms:
    
    \[
    P_{MOS}
    =
    P_C^{MOS}
    +
    P_C^{MOS,BD}
    +
    P_C^{MOS,3rd}
    +
    P_{on}
    +
    P_{off}
    +
    P_{rr}^{MOS,BD}
    \]

    \item Total diode loss is:
    \[
    P_{Diode}
    =
    P_C^{Diode}
    +
    P_{rr}^{Diode}
    \]

    \item Device losses affect junction temperature:
    \[
    T_J \uparrow
    \Rightarrow
    R_{DS(on)} \uparrow
    \Rightarrow
    P_{loss} \uparrow
    \]

    \item Therefore, electrothermal iteration is commonly used:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Estimate losses
        \item Compute junction temperature
        \item Update temperature-dependent parameters
        \item Recalculate losses
    \end{enumerate}

    \item The process repeats until thermal steady-state convergence is achieved.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================

\begin{itemize}
    \item Converter loss analysis is often divided into multiple time intervals.
    \item Example intervals:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item ON-state conduction
        \item Turn-OFF transition
        \item OFF-state blocking
        \item Turn-ON transition
    \end{itemize}

    \item In each interval:
    \begin{itemize}

        \item Junction temperature changes
        \item Device parameters vary
    \end{itemize}

    \item Accurate loss analysis therefore requires:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Time-domain waveform analysis
        \item Temperature-dependent device models
        \item Switching-energy characterization
    \end{itemize}

\end{itemize}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{GaN as a Wide Bandgap Semiconductor}

\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN belongs to the III-nitride semiconductor family, together with AlN and InN.
    \item Compared with silicon, GaN has:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item wider bandgap,
        \item higher critical electric field,
        \item higher electron saturation velocity,
        \item strong polarization effects.
    \end{itemize}

    \item These material properties allow GaN devices to support high-voltage and high-frequency operation simultaneously.
    \item GaN is a direct-bandgap material, which also explains its importance in optoelectronic devices such as LEDs.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Breakdown Voltage and Cutoff Frequency Trade-Off}

\begin{itemize}
    \item In high-frequency power devices, there is a fundamental trade-off between blocking voltage and carrier transit time.
    \item For a carrier path length $l$, the breakdown voltage is related to the critical electric field:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{V}} = E_{\mathrm{c}} l
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The cutoff frequency is related to the carrier saturation velocity:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
f_{\mathrm{T}} = \frac{1}{2\pi \tau}
= \frac{v_{\mathrm{sat}}}{2\pi l}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Therefore, the product $B_{\mathrm{V}} f_{\mathrm{T}}$ is mainly determined by the material.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Johnson Limit: Why GaN Is Attractive}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Multiplying breakdown voltage and cutoff frequency gives:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{V}} f_{\mathrm{T}}
=
\frac{E_{\mathrm{c}} v_{\mathrm{sat}}}{2\pi}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item A material with high $E_{\mathrm{c}}$ can support a high electric field before avalanche breakdown.
    \item A material with high $v_{\mathrm{sat}}$ allows faster carrier transport and higher frequency operation.
    \item GaN has both high critical electric field and high saturation velocity.
    \item This is why GaN is suitable for high-voltage and high-frequency power devices, especially when compact converter size is required.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Central point}
GaN is not only a “fast switch”; its advantage comes from the simultaneous combination of high breakdown field and high carrier velocity.
\end{block}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{III-Nitride Material System}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN, AlN, and InN form an important III-nitride material system.
    \item By alloying these materials, such as AlGaN or InGaN, the bandgap and lattice constant can be engineered.
    \item This allows heterostructures such as AlGaN/GaN to be designed for high-density carrier generation.
    \item The wurtzite crystal structure is central to the polarization behavior of GaN-based devices.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{GaN Crystal Polarity: Ga-Face and N-Face}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wurtzite GaN has a hexagonal crystal structure.
    \item Along the c-axis, the crystal can terminate with either:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Ga-face polarity,
        \item N-face polarity.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Because Ga and N atoms have different electronegativity, the crystal has an internal polarization direction.
    \item This asymmetry is a key difference between GaN-based devices and conventional silicon devices.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Spontaneous and Piezoelectric Polarization}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN-based heterostructures exhibit two important polarization effects:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item spontaneous polarization,
        \item piezoelectric polarization.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Spontaneous polarization exists because of the non-centrosymmetric wurtzite crystal structure.
    \item Piezoelectric polarization appears when mechanical strain changes the bond angles and charge distribution.
    \item In AlGaN/GaN, lattice mismatch produces strain and therefore additional polarization charge.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Polarization-Induced 2DEG}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item At the AlGaN/GaN interface, polarization discontinuity creates fixed sheet charge.
    \item Free electrons accumulate near the interface to neutralize this charge.
    \item These electrons form a two-dimensional electron gas, commonly called 2DEG.
    \item The 2DEG has high sheet carrier density and high mobility, enabling low on-resistance in lateral GaN HEMTs.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Key idea}
Unlike a conventional MOS channel, the conducting channel in GaN HEMTs can exist without intentional doping.
\end{block}

\begin{frame}{AlGaN/GaN HEMT: Normally-On Nature}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A standard AlGaN/GaN HEMT naturally contains a 2DEG at the heterointerface.
    \item Therefore, the device can conduct at zero gate bias.
    \item This is called a normally-on or depletion-mode device.
    \item For power converters, normally-off operation is preferred for fail-safe switching.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{T}}
=
\phi_{\mathrm{b}}
-
\Delta E_{\mathrm{c}}

        \item using p-GaN or p-AlGaN gate structures,
        \item introducing interface charge engineering.

    \item Each method shifts the threshold voltage but also affects:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item channel resistance,
        \item current capability,
        \item gate reliability,
        \item process complexity.

\begin{frame}{Natural Super Junction Structure in GaN}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.53\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In a natural super junction structure, nitride layers with different polarization are grown alternately.
    \item Electrons and holes are generated at different heterointerfaces.
    \item Under reverse bias, these stored charges can be depleted.
    \item Once depleted, the structure behaves as an insulator and can support high voltage.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
V_{0}
=
\frac{N_{\mathrm{s}}}{C_{0}}
=
\frac{N_{\mathrm{s}} C_{\mathrm{GaN}} C_{\mathrm{AlGaN}}}
{C_{\mathrm{GaN}} + C_{\mathrm{AlGaN}}}
\end{equation}

\begin{frame}{On-Resistance and Breakdown Voltage}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In lateral GaN FETs, the on-resistance can be separated into channel and drift components.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{on}} = R_{\mathrm{ch}} + R_{\mathrm{d}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{ch}}
=
\frac{L_{\mathrm{g}}}{W_{\mathrm{g}} q \mu N_{\mathrm{s}}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{d}}
=
\frac{L_{\mathrm{d}}}{W_{\mathrm{g}} q \mu N_{\mathrm{s}}}
=
\frac{1}{W_{\mathrm{g}} q \mu N_{\mathrm{s}}}
\left(\frac{B_{\mathrm{V}}}{E_{\mathrm{c}}}\right)
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item At low voltage, channel resistance may dominate.
    \item At high voltage, drift resistance becomes more important.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Design Takeaways for GaN Power Devices}
\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN power devices are enabled by wide bandgap material properties and polarization-induced carrier generation.
    \item The AlGaN/GaN heterostructure forms a high-density 2DEG without intentional channel doping.
    \item Standard GaN HEMTs are naturally normally-on; power converters generally require normally-off device structures.
    \item High-frequency operation is possible because of high saturation velocity and low device capacitance.
    \item High-voltage operation is supported by the high critical electric field of GaN.
    \item Practical GaN converter design must carefully manage gate drive, parasitic inductance, overvoltage, EMI, and thermal stress.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Key message}
GaN devices are polarization-engineered wide-bandgap devices, not simply as faster MOSFETs.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Evolution of Low-Voltage MOSFET Structures}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.5\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Reducing on-resistance in low-voltage devices requires increasing channel packing density.
    
    \item Silicon MOSFET structures evolved from:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item planar DMOS,
        \item trench-gate MOSFETs,
        \item fully self-aligned trench structures.
    \end{itemize}

    \item The objective is to reduce channel resistance while maintaining sufficient blocking capability.
    
    \item Similar scaling concepts are also important in GaN device engineering.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{DC Loss and AC Loss in Switching Devices}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Converter efficiency depends on both:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item DC conduction loss,
        \item AC switching loss.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Conduction loss is mainly related to device on-resistance:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Loss}_{\mathrm{DC}}
\propto
R_{\mathrm{on}} W_{\mathrm{g}}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item AC switching loss is strongly influenced by gate charge:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Loss}_{\mathrm{AC}}
\propto
\frac{Q_{\mathrm{g}}}{W_{\mathrm{g}}}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Therefore, minimizing the product $R_{\mathrm{on}}Q_{\mathrm{g}}$ is one of the key design targets in high-frequency power devices.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Lateral Versus Vertical Device Structures}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Most commercial GaN HEMTs are lateral devices.
    
    \item In lateral structures:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item current flows parallel to the wafer surface,
        \item the 2DEG channel provides low channel resistance.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Vertical devices conduct current through the bulk material.
    
    \item Vertical structures offer:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item higher packing density,
        \item higher current capability,
        \item improved high-voltage scalability.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Gate Charge and Switching Waveforms}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Gate charge strongly influences switching speed and switching loss.
    
    \item During turn-on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item gate voltage rises,
        \item drain voltage falls,
        \item drain current increases.
    \end{itemize}

    \item The Miller plateau appears because the gate-drain capacitance must be charged while $V_{\mathrm{DS}}$ changes.
    
    \item The total gate charge consists of:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item channel charge,
        \item switching charge,

\begin{frame}{Efficiency Versus Switching Frequency}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN devices enable operation at significantly higher switching frequency than conventional Si devices.
    
    \item However, switching frequency increases AC switching loss.
    
    \item Devices with lower $R_{\mathrm{on}}Q_{\mathrm{g}}$ maintain higher efficiency at MHz frequencies.
    
    \item This is one of the reasons why GaN is attractive for compact DC--DC converters and MHz resonant converters.
\end{itemize}

Reducing only $R_{\mathrm{on}}$ is not sufficient for high-frequency operation; gate charge must also be minimized.

\begin{frame}{Optimum Device Size for High-Frequency Switching}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Increasing gate width reduces conduction loss because $R_{\mathrm{on}}$ decreases.
    
    \item However, larger gate width also increases:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item capacitance,
        \item gate charge,
        \item switching loss.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Therefore, there exists an optimum device size where:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item DC loss and AC loss are balanced,
        \item total converter loss becomes minimum.
    \end{itemize}

    \item The optimum gate width shifts toward smaller devices at higher switching frequency.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Breakdown Voltage and Drift-Region Resistance}

\begin{itemize}
    \item In one-dimensional drift-region analysis, the electric field is:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
E_{\mathrm{c}}
=
\frac{qN_{\mathrm{d}}}{\varepsilon_{\mathrm{GaN}}}
W_{\mathrm{d}}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The breakdown voltage becomes:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
B_{\mathrm{V}}
=
\frac{1}{2}E_{\mathrm{c}}W_{\mathrm{d}}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The epitaxial drift-region resistance is approximately:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{epi}}
\propto
\frac{B_{\mathrm{V}}^{2}}
{\mu E_{\mathrm{c}}^{3}}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Because GaN has high $E_{\mathrm{c}}$, it can achieve lower drift resistance than Si at the same blocking voltage.
\end{itemize}


\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Conductivity Modulation in Silicon Devices}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Silicon high-voltage devices often use conductivity modulation to reduce drift-region resistance.
    
    \item Examples include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item GTO,
        \item SI thyristor,
        \item IGBT.
    \end{itemize}

    \item Minority-carrier injection increases carrier density in the drift region and reduces conduction loss.
    
    \item However, stored charge slows switching speed and increases turn-off loss.
    
    \item GaN devices are attractive because they can achieve low resistance without relying heavily on minority-carrier storage.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Vertical GaN Devices and CAVET Structures}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Most commercial GaN devices today are lateral HEMTs.
    
    \item For very high-voltage operation, vertical structures are increasingly important.
    
    \item One important concept is the CAVET:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Current Aperture Vertical Electron Transistor.
    \end{itemize}

    \item In a CAVET:
    \begin{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Basic Depletion-Mode GaN HEMT Structure}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A basic GaN HEMT consists of source, drain, and gate electrodes formed on an AlGaN/GaN heterostructure.
    \item The AlGaN/GaN interface naturally forms a two-dimensional electron gas, or 2DEG.
    \item In a depletion-mode device, the 2DEG is present at zero gate bias.
    \item Therefore, the device is normally ON unless a negative gate voltage depletes the channel.
    \item This behavior is useful physically, but inconvenient for fail-safe power converter operation.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/GaN_depletion_mode.png}
\caption{Depletion-mode GaN HEMT operation with negative gate bias; adapted from “Lidow, Alex, et al. GaN power devices for efficient power conversion. John Wiley \& Sons” 2025.}
\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Schottky-Gate and Insulated-Gate D-Mode HEMTs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early GaN HEMTs commonly used a Schottky gate directly deposited on the AlGaN barrier.
    \item The Schottky gate controls the 2DEG by modulating the electric field through the AlGaN layer.
    \item A second option is an insulated gate, where a dielectric layer separates the metal gate from the semiconductor.
    \item Insulated-gate structures are closer to MOSFET operation and can reduce gate leakage.
    \item However, gate-dielectric quality and interface reliability become critical issues.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/GaN_schottky_insulated.png}

\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Normally-Off GaN Is Required}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In power converters, normally-off behavior is strongly preferred for safety.
    \item If the gate driver fails, a normally-on device may conduct unintentionally and create a short circuit.
    \item Enhancement-mode GaN devices are OFF at zero gate bias.
    \item A positive gate voltage is required to restore or induce the conducting channel.
    \item This makes enhancement-mode GaN more suitable for practical inverter and converter systems.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/GaN_enhancement_mode.png}
\caption{Enhancement-mode GaN device: zero-bias channel depletion and positive-bias conduction; adapted from “Lidow, Alex, et al. GaN power devices for efficient power conversion. John Wiley \& Sons” 2025.}
\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Recessed-Gate Enhancement-Mode GaN}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item A recessed-gate structure is obtained by etching part of the AlGaN barrier under the gate.
    \item Thinning the AlGaN barrier reduces the polarization-induced charge under the gate.
    \item If the barrier is sufficiently thin, the 2DEG is depleted at zero gate voltage.
    \item When positive gate bias is applied, electrons are attracted back to the interface and the channel is restored.
    \item The main challenge is precise etch control and reliable gate-interface formation.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/GaN_recessed_gate.png}

\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Fluorine-Implanted Enhancement-Mode GaN}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Fluorine implantation introduces negative fixed charge into the AlGaN barrier.
    \item These negative charges repel electrons and locally deplete the 2DEG under the gate.
    \item A Schottky gate can then be used to reconstruct the 2DEG when a positive voltage is applied.
    \item This method shifts the threshold voltage in the positive direction.
    \item The key concern is long-term threshold stability and trapping-related reliability.
\end{itemize}

\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{p-GaN Gate Enhancement-Mode Structure}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In the p-GaN gate structure, a p-type GaN layer is grown on top of the AlGaN barrier.
    \item The positive charge in the p-GaN region depletes the 2DEG below the gate at zero bias.
    \item This creates normally-off operation.
    \item p-GaN gate HEMTs are widely used in commercial enhancement-mode GaN power devices.
    \item Their design must consider gate voltage limits, gate current, and threshold-voltage stability.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Hybrid Normally-Off GaN Structures}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Hybrid normally-off solutions combine a low-voltage Si MOSFET with a high-voltage depletion-mode GaN HEMT.
    \item In a cascode structure, the Si MOSFET controls the source potential of the GaN HEMT.
    \item When the Si MOSFET is OFF, the GaN gate-source voltage becomes negative and the GaN HEMT turns OFF.
    \item When the Si MOSFET is ON, the GaN HEMT conducts together with the MOSFET.
    \item This approach enables normally-off behavior using a naturally normally-on GaN transistor.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Cascode GaN: Added Resistance Contribution}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In a cascode GaN device, the low-voltage Si MOSFET adds some on-resistance.
    \item At higher blocking voltage, the GaN HEMT resistance dominates and the MOSFET contribution becomes relatively small.
    \item Therefore, cascode structures are more attractive at higher voltage ratings.
    \item At lower voltage ratings, the Si MOSFET contribution becomes more significant.
    \item This is why device selection must consider the complete series conduction path.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Reverse Conduction in Enhancement-Mode GaN}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Enhancement-mode GaN HEMTs can conduct current in both forward and reverse directions.
    \item During reverse conduction, the device does not rely on minority-carrier diode conduction.
    \item Instead, when the drain voltage becomes sufficiently lower than the gate voltage, the 2DEG is restored.
    \item Current can then flow through the channel in the reverse direction.
    \item This gives GaN devices diode-like functionality without conventional reverse-recovery charge.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/GaN_reverse_conduction.png}
\caption{Forward and reverse current flow in enhancement-mode GaN HEMT; adapted “Lidow, Alex, et al. GaN power devices for efficient power conversion. John Wiley \& Sons” 2025.}
\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Reverse Conduction: Difference from Silicon MOSFETs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In a silicon MOSFET, reverse current often flows through the intrinsic body diode during dead time.
    \item This body diode involves minority-carrier storage and therefore has reverse-recovery behavior.
    \item In an enhancement-mode GaN HEMT, reverse conduction is channel-based rather than body-diode-based.
    \item As a result, GaN devices can reduce reverse-recovery-related switching loss.
    \item However, the reverse voltage drop can be high if the gate is not properly driven during dead time.
    \item Synchronous gate control is therefore important for achieving low reverse conduction loss.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Key message}
GaN reverse conduction is not identical to MOSFET body-diode conduction; it is mainly controlled by channel reformation and gate bias conditions.
\end{block}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Comparison of Enhancement-Mode GaN Approaches}
\begin{columns}
    \column{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Recessed gate}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Directly depletes 2DEG under gate.
        \item Sensitive to etch damage and interface quality.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Fluorine implantation}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Uses negative fixed charge.
        \item Threshold stability can be challenging.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{p-GaN gate}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Commercially important solution.
        \item Requires careful gate-voltage limitation.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\column{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Cascode / hybrid}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Uses low-voltage Si MOSFET with d-mode GaN.
        \item Compatible with standard gate driving.
        \item Adds series resistance and package complexity.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Direct-drive hybrid}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Gives more control of GaN switching.
        \item Requires more complex gate-drive supply.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Design Takeaways for GaN Transistor Structures}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The natural AlGaN/GaN 2DEG makes GaN HEMTs highly conductive but often normally-on.
    \item Power electronics generally requires normally-off devices for safe startup and fault handling.
    \item Enhancement-mode GaN can be realized by recessing the gate, implanting charge, using p-GaN gates, or applying hybrid/cascode concepts.
    \item Each method trades off threshold voltage, on-resistance, gate reliability, manufacturability, and switching behavior.
    \item Reverse conduction in GaN is channel-based and can provide fast operation with negligible reverse-recovery charge.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Key message}
GaN transistor design is fundamentally about controlling the 2DEG: forming it for low loss, depleting it for normally-off behavior, and restoring it during controlled conduction.
\end{block}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{GaN Transistor Electrical Characteristics}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Electrical characteristics define how a GaN transistor behaves in practical converter operation.
    \item Important parameters include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item drain-source breakdown voltage,
        \item drain leakage current,
        \item on-resistance,
        \item threshold voltage,
        \item capacitance and gate charge,
        \item reverse conduction behavior.
    \end{itemize}
    \item These parameters determine conduction loss, switching loss, safe operating range, and gate-driver requirements.
    \item GaN devices must therefore be evaluated both statically and dynamically.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Drain-Source Voltage Rating}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The drain-source voltage rating defines the maximum voltage the device can block between drain and source.
    \item In the OFF state, the GaN transistor must withstand drain-source voltage without forming an unwanted current path.
    \item Breakdown can occur if the electric field exceeds the critical field of:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item GaN,
        \item AlGaN,
        \item gate dielectric,
        \item passivation or insulating layers.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Device geometry, field plates, and surface passivation strongly influence the practical voltage rating.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/GaN_reverse_bias.png}
\caption{Reverse bias in e-mode and d-mode GaN transistors; adapted from “Lidow, Alex, et al. GaN power devices for efficient power conversion. John Wiley \& Sons” 2025.}
\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Electric-Field Distribution During Blocking}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During blocking, high electric fields appear near the drain-side edge of the gate.
    \item Closely spaced electric-field contour lines indicate strong local field concentration.
    \item If the field becomes too high, breakdown may initiate locally before the whole device reaches its ideal material limit.
    \item Field management is therefore central in GaN transistor design.
    \item Field plates and optimized gate-drain spacing are commonly used to reduce electric-field crowding.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.42\textwidth}

\begin{frame}{Leakage Current Components}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Even in the OFF state, a small leakage current flows between device terminals.
    \item The total drain-source leakage may include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item drain-to-source leakage,
        \item drain-to-gate leakage,
        \item drain-to-substrate leakage.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Leakage current increases with blocking voltage and temperature.
    \item For high-efficiency converters, leakage loss can be important during long standby or high-voltage blocking intervals.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Temperature Dependence of Leakage Current}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drain-source leakage current is strongly temperature dependent.
    \item Higher temperature increases carrier generation and thermally activated leakage paths.
    \item Device-to-device variation is also important, especially near the maximum rated voltage.
    \item In datasheets, leakage is usually specified at defined:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item drain-source voltage,
        \item gate-source voltage,
        \item junction temperature.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Comparing devices requires careful attention to these test conditions.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Datasheet Ratings: $B_{\mathrm{VDSS}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{DSS}}$}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Datasheets usually separate absolute maximum ratings from static electrical characteristics.
    \item $B_{\mathrm{VDSS}}$ defines the drain-source blocking voltage under specified test current.
    \item $I_{\mathrm{DSS}}$ defines leakage current under specified blocking voltage and gate bias.
    \item Different manufacturers may specify these values at different voltages, currents, and temperatures.
    \item Therefore, direct comparison requires normalization of test conditions.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{On-Resistance Components in GaN HEMTs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The on-resistance of a GaN transistor is the sum of several resistance components.
    \item Important contributions include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item source and drain contact resistance,
        \item access-region 2DEG resistance,
        \item gate-region 2DEG resistance,
        \item parasitic metal and package resistance.
    \end{itemize}
    \item The 2DEG resistance depends on electron mobility, sheet density, and conduction width.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
R_{\mathrm{HEMT}}
=
2R_{\mathrm{C}} + R_{\mathrm{2DEG}} + R_{\mathrm{2DEG(gate)}}
\end{equation}

\begin{frame}{Temperature Dependence of $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ increases with junction temperature.
    \item In GaN HEMTs, this increase is strongly influenced by the temperature dependence of 2DEG mobility.
    \item Contact and metal resistances also change with temperature, but the 2DEG contribution can dominate.
    \item For converter design, $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ should be evaluated at expected operating junction temperature, not only at $25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{cond}}
=
I_{\mathrm{D,rms}}^{2}R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}(T_{\mathrm{j}})
\end{equation}

\begin{frame}{$R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ Dependence on Gate Voltage}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The on-resistance decreases as gate-source voltage increases.
    \item At low gate voltage, the channel under the gate is not fully enhanced.
    \item Once the 2DEG is fully restored, further gate-voltage increase gives only limited reduction in resistance.
    \item This is why GaN devices require a well-defined positive gate-drive voltage.
    \item Under-driving the gate increases conduction loss and can raise junction temperature.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Datasheet Specification of $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Datasheets specify $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ at a defined gate voltage, drain current, and temperature.
    \item For low-voltage GaN, the specified gate voltage may be around $5~\mathrm{V}$.
    \item For higher-voltage GaN devices, the specified gate voltage may be different, such as $6~\mathrm{V}$.
    \item Maximum values are more useful for thermal design than typical values.
    \item Always check whether the datasheet value is measured at $25^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ or elevated temperature.

\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage in GaN Transistors}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Threshold voltage is the gate-source voltage at which drain current begins to flow under a defined test condition.
    \item Enhancement-mode GaN has a positive threshold voltage.
    \item Depletion-mode GaN has a negative threshold voltage.
    \item In GaN devices, threshold voltage depends on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item polarization-induced charge,
        \item AlGaN barrier design,
        \item gate metal or p-GaN gate structure,
        \item temperature.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage: GaN Versus Si MOSFET}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN threshold voltage is usually low compared with many silicon MOSFETs.
    \item This makes gate-drive noise immunity especially important.
    \item Compared with Si MOSFETs, GaN threshold voltage can show smaller temperature variation.
    \item However, the small absolute threshold margin means that:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item false turn-on must be avoided,
        \item negative gate undershoot must be controlled,
        \item gate-driver layout must be very compact.
    \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Datasheet Specification of Threshold Voltage}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Threshold voltage is specified using a small drain current criterion.
    \item Therefore, it should not be confused with the recommended gate voltage for low-loss conduction.
    \item For example, a device may have $V_{\mathrm{th}}$ near $1$--$2~\mathrm{V}$ but still require $5$--$6~\mathrm{V}$ for full enhancement.
    \item In converter design, threshold voltage is mainly useful for:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item noise-margin assessment,
        \item false turn-on analysis,
        \item gate-driver protection design.

\begin{frame}{Capacitances in GaN Transistors}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN transistor switching behavior is strongly influenced by terminal capacitances.
    \item The main intrinsic capacitances are:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item gate-source capacitance $C_{\mathrm{GS}}$,
        \item gate-drain capacitance $C_{\mathrm{GD}}$,
        \item drain-source capacitance $C_{\mathrm{DS}}$.
    \end{itemize}
    \item Datasheets often report:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{iss}} = C_{\mathrm{GS}} + C_{\mathrm{GD}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
C_{\mathrm{oss}} = C_{\mathrm{DS}} + C_{\mathrm{GD}},
\qquad
C_{\mathrm{rss}} = C_{\mathrm{GD}}
\end{equation}

\begin{frame}{Voltage Dependence of GaN Capacitances}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN capacitances are strongly voltage dependent.
    \item As $V_{\mathrm{DS}}$ increases, the output capacitance usually decreases.
    \item This occurs because the 2DEG near the drain is depleted and the effective charge-storage region changes.
    \item $C_{\mathrm{oss}}$ is especially important because it affects:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item soft-switching energy,
        \item voltage transition time,
        \item resonant ringing,
        \item switching loss.

\begin{frame}{Gate Charge and Miller Plateau}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gate charge describes the amount of charge required to move the device from OFF state to ON state.
    \item The gate-charge curve links charge to gate-source voltage.
    \item The nearly flat region corresponds to the Miller interval, where drain-source voltage changes.
    \item Lower gate charge allows faster switching and lower gate-drive loss.
    \item However, very fast switching increases sensitivity to parasitic inductance and EMI.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
Q = \int i_{\mathrm{G}}(t)\,dt
\end{equation}

\begin{frame}{Reverse Conduction in GaN HEMTs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item GaN HEMTs do not have a conventional p-n body diode like Si MOSFETs.
    \item Reverse current can still flow through the 2DEG channel.
    \item If $V_{\mathrm{GS}}$ is high enough, reverse conduction occurs with nearly the same on-resistance as forward conduction.
    \item If the gate is OFF, reverse current requires a larger source-to-drain voltage to re-establish channel conduction.
    \item This behavior is very important during dead time in half-bridge converters.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Reverse Conduction Versus Si MOSFET Body Diode}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item In Si MOSFETs, reverse current usually flows through the intrinsic body diode during dead time.
    \item This diode stores minority charge and creates reverse-recovery loss.
    \item In enhancement-mode GaN, there is no minority-carrier reverse recovery.
    \item Therefore, the specified reverse-recovery charge can be effectively zero.
    \item The trade-off is that reverse voltage drop can be high if the gate is not properly driven.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Design Takeaways from GaN Electrical Characteristics}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $B_{\mathrm{VDSS}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{DSS}}$ define voltage blocking and leakage behavior.
    \item $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ determines conduction loss and increases with temperature.
    \item Threshold voltage is low, so gate-drive noise immunity is critical.
    \item Capacitances and gate charge determine switching speed, switching loss, and EMI sensitivity.
    \item Reverse conduction is channel-based and avoids conventional reverse recovery.
    \item Proper GaN converter design requires simultaneous optimization of device selection, gate drive, layout, dead time, and thermal management.
\end{itemize}

\begin{block}{Key message}
GaN electrical characteristics cannot be judged from $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ alone; voltage rating, leakage, threshold, capacitance, charge, and reverse conduction must be evaluated together.
\end{block}
\end{frame}
