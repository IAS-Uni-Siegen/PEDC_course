\begin{frame}{Why Do We Need Wide-Bandgap Devices?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Conventional Si devices have enabled modern power electronics, but they face practical limits at high voltage, high temperature, and high switching frequency.
    \item In many converters, the device is no longer the only limitation; passive components, cooling system, EMI filters, and packaging dominate size, weight, and cost.
    \item Wide-bandgap (WBG) devices allow faster switching and lower losses, enabling compact and efficient converters.
    \item The major motivation is not simply to replace Si devices, but to redesign the whole converter around higher frequency, higher temperature, and higher power density.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Do We Need Wide-Bandgap Devices?}

\begin{block}{Typical Motivating Examples}
\begin{itemize}
    \item EV traction inverters and on-board chargers
    \item Fast chargers and high-power DC--DC converters
    \item Low-inductance and high-speed motor drives
    \item Aerospace and integrated motor drives
    \item Renewable-energy converters and PV inverters
    \item Data-center and high-frequency power supplies
\end{itemize}
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Key Design Promise}
Lower loss + higher switching frequency + higher temperature capability
$\Rightarrow$ smaller passives, smaller cooling system, and higher power density.
\end{block}

\end{frame}


\begin{frame}{Why Are They Called ``Wide-Bandgap'' Devices?}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The bandgap $E_g$ is the energy difference between the valence band and the conduction band of a semiconductor.
    \item Silicon has a bandgap of about $1.1$ eV, whereas SiC and GaN have bandgaps above $3$ eV.
    \item A larger bandgap usually means lower intrinsic carrier concentration, lower leakage current, and better high-temperature blocking capability.
    \item More importantly for power devices, WBG materials can sustain a much higher critical electric field before breakdown.
    \item This allows a thinner and more highly doped drift region, reducing specific on-resistance for a given blocking voltage.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Crystal Structure of Silicon Carbide}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Silicon carbide (SiC) is a compound semiconductor made from silicon and carbon atoms in an approximately 1:1 ratio.
    \item Each Si atom forms covalent bonds with four neighbouring C atoms, and each C atom bonds with four neighbouring Si atoms.
    \item The local bonding is approximately tetrahedral and is associated with $sp^3$ hybridization.
    \item The strong Si--C bond is one of the physical reasons for SiC having a large bandgap, high breakdown field, and high-temperature capability.
    \item Unlike Si, SiC does not have only one common crystal form; it appears in several polytypes due to different stacking sequences.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{SiC Polytypes: 3C, 4H, and 6H}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC crystals are formed by stacking Si--C bilayers in different sequences.
    \item The notation \textbf{C}, \textbf{H}, and \textbf{R} denotes cubic, hexagonal, and rhombohedral crystal systems.
    \item Common SiC polytypes include \textbf{3C-SiC}, \textbf{4H-SiC}, and \textbf{6H-SiC}.
    \item The number indicates the number of Si--C double atomic layers in one repeating unit along the stacking direction.
    \item For power devices, \textbf{4H-SiC} is preferred because it provides a favourable combination of bandgap, carrier transport, breakdown field, and mature wafer technology.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Breakdown Field and Intrinsic Carrier \\Concentration of SiC}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC has a breakdown field of approximately $2.5~\mathrm{MV/cm}$, about ten times higher than Si.
    \item Therefore, for the same blocking voltage, the SiC drift region can be thinner and more heavily doped than in a Si device.
    \item This reduces the drift-region resistance, which is usually the dominant part of $R_{\mathrm{DS(on)}}$ in high-voltage MOSFETs.
    \item SiC also has a much lower intrinsic carrier concentration than Si: $n_i(\mathrm{SiC}) \ll n_i(\mathrm{Si})$.
    \item The lower $n_i$ allows SiC devices to retain blocking capability at much higher junction temperatures.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Commercial SiC MOSFET Device Structures}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Commercial SiC MOSFETs use vertical current flow so that high current and high blocking voltage can be obtained within a practical chip area.
    \item Lightly doped $n^-$ drift region supports blocking voltage, while the channel and source/body regions control the conduction path.
    \item Planar SiC MOSFETs were the first commercial technology, but trench structures are widely used to reduce channel resistance and increase cell density.
    \item In trench MOSFETs, electric-field crowding at the trench bottom is a critical reliability concern for the gate oxide.
    \item Shielded or double-trench structures reduce the electric field at critical oxide corners and improve reliability, switching performance, and power density.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Reverse Blocking Characteristics of SiC Diodes}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During reverse bias, the cathode is at a higher potential than the anode and only a small reverse leakage current flows.
    
    \item The reverse current $I_R$ depends on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reverse voltage $V_R$
        \item Junction temperature $T_j$
    \end{itemize}

    \item At low reverse voltage:$I_R \approx \text{pA to nA range}$ indicating effective blocking operation.

    \item As $V_R$ approaches the breakdown region, avalanche multiplication causes a rapid increase in current.

    \item SiC devices typically exhibit:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item High breakdown voltage margin
        \item Low leakage current
        \item Improved high-temperature blocking capability
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Forward Conduction Characteristics}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under forward bias, current flows from anode to cathode once the forward voltage exceeds the conduction threshold.
    
    \item The forward conduction behaviour is represented by the: $I_F - V_F $ characteristic.

    \item At low forward voltage: $I_F \approx 0$, diode remains OFF.

    \item Beyond the knee voltage, the current increases rapidly with applied voltage.

    \item The forward characteristics vary with junction temperature:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Forward voltage decreases with increasing $T_j$
        \item Temperature coefficient changes with operating current
    \end{itemize}

    \item The near-zero temperature coefficient region helps current sharing during parallel operation.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Junction Capacitance and Charge}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Under reverse bias, a depletion region forms between the p-region and n-region.
    
    \item This depletion region behaves similarly to a voltage-dependent capacitor.

    \item The junction capacitance decreases with increasing reverse voltage: $C_j \downarrow \quad \text{as} \quad V_R \uparrow$.
    \item The stored junction charge is obtained from: $Q_C = \int_0^{V_R} C(V_R)\, dV_R$.
    \item Lower junction capacitance is beneficial because it:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reduces switching losses
        \item Improves high-frequency performance
        \item Reduces reverse recovery effects
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Junction Capacitance Energy and Practical Implications}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}

    \item The energy stored in junction capacitance is:
    \[
        E_C = \int_0^{V_R} C(V_R)\,V_R\,dV_R

    \begin{itemize}
        \item High-frequency switching
        \item Hard commutation
        \item Fast transient operation
    \end{itemize}

    \item Lower stored energy in SiC diodes contributes to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reduced switching loss
        \item Faster switching transitions
        \item Lower reverse recovery stress

\begin{frame}{Power Dissipation in SiC Power Diodes}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During operation, a SiC diode dissipates power mainly through forward conduction and switching-related capacitive effects.
    \item The junction temperature rises until thermal equilibrium is reached between generated heat and removed heat.
    \item With case temperature $T_C$ specified, the steady-state thermal relation is
    \[
        T_J - T_C = P_{\mathrm{tot}}(T_C)\,R_{\mathrm{th}(JC)}
    \]
    \item Therefore, the maximum allowable power dissipation decreases as $T_C$ increases.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Forward Current Limit and Surge Capability}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The allowable forward current is limited by the maximum dissipated power, duty cycle, and forward voltage drop.
    \item For duty cycle $D$, the current limit can be estimated as
    \[
        I_F =
        \frac{P_{\mathrm{tot}}(T_C)/D}
        {V_F\!\left(T_{J(\max)},I_F\right)}
    \]
    \item Datasheets therefore provide $I_F$--$T_C$ curves for different duty cycles.
    \item Surge forward current $I_{FSM}$ defines the maximum non-repetitive half-sinusoidal current the diode can withstand.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Forward Current Limit and Surge Capability}
\begin{columns}
\begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The $i^2t$ rating measures short-time thermal withstand capability:
    \[
        i^2t=\int_0^{10\,\mathrm{ms}}
        \left(I_{FSM}\sin{\omega t}\right)^2dt
        \approx 0.005\,I_{FSM}^2
    \]
    \item These properties make SiC diodes highly suitable for:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item High-frequency converters
        \item EV traction systems
        \item PFC stages
        \item Renewable-energy converters
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\end{column}

\begin{column}{0.47\textwidth}
\begin{block}{Design Meaning}
\small
At higher case or ambient temperature, less power can be dissipated before the junction reaches $T_{J(\max)}$.
\end{block}

\begin{block}{Practical Point}
\small
Surge ratings are strongly dependent on pulse duration, repetition, and initial junction temperature; they should not be compared blindly across datasheets.
\end{block}

\end{column}
\end{columns}

\begin{frame}{SiC Power MOSFETs: Motivation and Challenges}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs enable:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item High blocking voltage
        \item Low specific ON-resistance
        \item High-temperature operation
        \item Fast switching capability
    \end{itemize}

    \item Baliga's figure of merit for 4H-SiC is more than 2000$\times$ higher than silicon.

    \item However, early commercialization faced major challenges:
    \begin{itemize}

        \item Low channel mobility
        \item High electric field stress in gate oxide
        \item Reliability concerns during blocking operation
    \end{itemize}

    \item These challenges motivated the development of advanced SiC MOSFET structures.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Reach-Through Limitation in SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item During blocking operation, the depletion region expands into the P-base region.

    \item If the P-base becomes fully depleted, reach-through breakdown occurs.

    \item Minimum P-base thickness:
    \[
    t_P=\frac{\varepsilon_s E_C}{qN_A}
    \]

    \item Due to the large critical electric field of SiC:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Higher P-base doping is required
        \item Threshold voltage increases significantly
    \end{itemize}

    \item This creates a trade-off between:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Blocking capability
        \item Threshold voltage
        \item Channel resistance
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage in SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The threshold voltage is the gate voltage required to form a strong inversion channel.

    \item For SiC planar MOSFETs:
    \[
    V_{TH}=
    \frac{\sqrt{4\varepsilon_skTN_A\ln(N_A/n_i)}}{C_{ox}}
    +\frac{2kT}{q}\ln\left(\frac{N_A}{n_i}\right)
    \]

    \item SiC devices typically exhibit:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Higher threshold voltage
        \item Lower channel mobility
    \end{itemize}

    \item Causes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Wide bandgap
        \item Very low intrinsic carrier concentration
        \item High P-base doping requirement
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Gate Oxide Electric Field in SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs experience very high electric field stress in the gate oxide during blocking operation.

    \item This can cause:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Oxide degradation
        \item Hot electron injection
        \item Threshold voltage shift
    \end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Accumulation-Mode SiC MOSFET}

\begin{frame}{Leakage Current and Breakdown Voltage}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.54\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item With \(V_{GS}=0\), the SiC MOSFET blocks the applied drain voltage \(V_{DS}\).

    \item A small leakage current \(I_{DSS}\) flows in the OFF-state.

    \item \(I_{DSS}\) increases with:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Increasing \(V_{DS}\)
        \item Increasing junction temperature \(T_J\)
    \end{itemize}

    \item At sufficiently high \(V_{DS}\), avalanche breakdown occurs:
    \[
    V_{DS} \rightarrow V_{(BR)DSS}
    \]

    \item SiC MOSFETs usually exhibit breakdown voltages significantly above their nominal ratings.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Temperature Dependence of Breakdown Voltage}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.55\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The breakdown voltage increases with junction temperature:
    \[
    V_{(BR)DSS} \uparrow \quad \text{as} \quad T_J \uparrow
    \]

    \item Higher temperature reduces carrier mobility due to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Lattice scattering
        \item Impurity scattering
    \end{itemize}

    \item Reduced mobility makes avalanche multiplication more difficult.

    \item The breakdown voltage is commonly defined at a specified leakage current:
    \[
    I_{(BR)DSS}
    \]

    \item Typical values of \(I_{(BR)DSS}\) range from:
    \[
    100~\mu A \; \text{to} \; 5~mA
    \]
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Power Dissipation and Drain Current}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Power losses increase the junction temperature of the MOSFET.

    \item The steady-state thermal relation is:
    \[
    T_J-T_C=P_{tot}(T_C)\cdot R_{th(JC)}
    \]

    \item As case temperature \(T_C\) increases:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Maximum allowable power decreases
        \item Maximum drain current decreases
    \end{itemize}

    \item Continuous drain current is approximated by:
    \[
    I_D=
    \sqrt{
    \frac{P_{tot}(T_C)}
    {R_{DS(on)}(T_{J(max)})}
    }
    \]
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage Variations and DIBL Effect}
\begin{columns}[T]

    \begin{itemize}

    \end{itemize}

    \item SiC MOSFETs show significant: $\text{Drain-Induced Barrier Lowering (DIBL)}$

    \item Increasing \(V_{DS}\) reduces: $V_{GS(th)}$

    \item Combined effects of high temperature and DIBL may lead to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Unintended turn-ON
        \item Shoot-through faults
        \item Increased switching losses
    \end{itemize}

    \item Careful gate driver and layout design are therefore essential.
\end{itemize}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Threshold Voltage Hysteresis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs exhibit threshold voltage hysteresis during repeated switching operation.

    \item The primary cause is the high density of interface traps at the SiC/SiO\(_2\) interface.

    \item Gate bias stress changes the trapped charge distribution:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Negative gate bias increases positive oxide traps
        \item Positive gate bias traps electrons in the oxide
    \end{itemize}

    \item As a result:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \(V_{GS(th)}\) shifts dynamically
        \item Switching characteristics become history dependent
        \item Measurement repeatability becomes challenging
    \end{itemize}

    \item Threshold voltage hysteresis strongly affects:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Short-circuit performance
        \item False turn-ON behavior
        \item Gate charge characteristics
        \item Reliability evaluation
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Dynamic Characteristics of SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The dynamic behavior of a SiC MOSFET is governed mainly by its parasitic capacitances between gate, drain, and source terminals.
    \item Unlike bipolar devices, there is no excess minority-carrier storage in the main MOSFET channel; therefore, the switching speed can be very high.
    \item The key capacitances are:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Gate-source capacitance: $C_{GS}$
        \item Gate-drain capacitance: $C_{GD}$
        \item Drain-source capacitance: $C_{DS}$
    \end{itemize}
    \item These capacitances are strongly nonlinear because the depletion region changes with $V_{DS}$.
    \item During switching, the measured voltage and current waveforms are therefore shaped by both the gate-drive circuit and the voltage-dependent capacitances.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Junction Capacitances of SiC MOSFET}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $C_{GS}$ consists mainly of the channel-gate oxide capacitance and the gate-source overlap capacitance.
    \item $C_{GD}$ is the feedback capacitance and is strongly related to the Miller effect.
    \item $C_{DS}$ is mainly associated with the drain-source pn-junction depletion region.
    \item Datasheets usually report:
    \begin{align*}
        C_{iss} &= C_{GS}+C_{GD} \\
        C_{oss} &= C_{DS}+C_{GD} \\
        C_{rss} &= C_{GD}
    \end{align*}
    \item $C_{rss}$ is especially important because it controls the drain-voltage transition during switching.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Voltage Dependence of Capacitances}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item The capacitance values are not constant during operation.
    \item As $V_{DS}$ increases, depletion region expands and junction capacitances decrease.
    \item $C_{iss}$ changes relatively little because it is dominated by $C_{GS}$.
    \item $C_{oss}$ and $C_{rss}$ show a stronger reduction with increasing $V_{DS}$.
    \item At high $V_{DS}$, the depletion region changes slowly; therefore, the capacitances become relatively constant.
    \item In practical switching analysis, the nonlinear capacitance behavior must be considered carefully.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Inductive Load Switching Circuit}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFET switching is commonly evaluated using an inductive-load circuit.
    \item The circuit includes:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item DC bus capacitor $C_{Bus}$
        \item Load inductance $L$
        \item Gate resistance $R_G$
        \item Power-loop inductance $L_{Loop}$
        \item Drive-loop inductance $L_{DRV}$
    \end{itemize}
    \item The upper device body diode or external diode conducts during freewheeling.
    \item During turn-ON, the lower MOSFET takes over current from the diode.
    \item This process creates reverse recovery, current overshoot, voltage ringing, and high $dv/dt$.
\end{itemize}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-ON Process: Initial Charging Stage}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Before turn-ON, the lower SiC MOSFET is OFF:
    \[
    I_{DS}=0,\qquad V_{DS}=V_{Bus}
    \]
    \item At $t_0$, the gate drive changes from $V_{DRV(off)}$ to $V_{DRV(on)}$.
    \item The gate current charges the input capacitance:
    \[
    I_G = I_{CGS}+I_{CGD}
    \]
    \item Since $V_{DS}$ is still almost constant, $C_{GS}$ and $C_{GD}$ are approximately constant in this interval.
    \item The gate voltage rises exponentially toward the applied drive voltage:
    \[
    V_{GS}(t)=V_{DRV(on)}
    -\left(V_{DRV(on)}-V_{DRV(off)}\right)
    e^{-\frac{t-t_0}{R_G C_{iss}}}
    \]
    \item When $V_{GS}$ reaches $V_{GS(th)}$, the MOSFET starts conducting and the turn-ON delay ends.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-ON Process: Current Rise and Reverse Recovery}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item After $V_{GS}>V_{GS(th)}$, channel current starts to increase.
    \item In the saturation region:
    \[
    I_{channel}=K\left(V_{GS}-V_{GS(th)}\right)^2
    \]
    \item Initially:$I_{DS}\approx I_{channel}$
    \item The increasing $I_{DS}$ generates voltage drop across loop inductance:
    \[
    \Delta V_{DS}=L_{Loop}\frac{dI_{DS}}{dt}
    \]
    \item When $I_{DS}$ exceeds the load current, the diode enters reverse recovery and produces current overshoot.
\end{itemize}

\column{0.44\textwidth}
\vspace{-0.25cm}
\begin{figure}
\centering

\caption{Turn-ON waveform with diode reverse recovery.}
\end{figure}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-ON Process: Miller Ramp and Voltage Fall}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During the voltage-fall interval, $V_{DS}$ decreases rapidly while $I_{DS}$ is already high.
    \item The drain-gate capacitance current becomes important:
    \[
    I_{CGD}=C_{GD}\frac{dV_{GD}}{dt}
    \]
    \item Since:
    \[
    V_{GD}=V_{GS}-V_{DS}
    \]
    and usually:
    \[
    \left|\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}\right| \gg \left|\frac{dV_{GS}}{dt}\right|
    \]
    the feedback current is approximately:
    \[
    I_{CGD}\approx C_{GD}\left|\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}\right|
    \]
    \item Unlike silicon MOSFETs, SiC MOSFETs may not show a perfectly flat Miller plateau.
    \item The gate voltage can continue rising slowly due to DIBL, low channel mobility, and nonlinear $C_{GD}$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-OFF Process of SiC MOSFET}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During turn-OFF, the gate drive changes from $V_{DRV(on)}$ to $V_{DRV(off)}$.
    \item The input capacitance is discharged through the gate resistance.
    \item Initially, $V_{DS}$ and $I_{DS}$ do not change significantly.
    \item As $V_{GS}$ decreases, $R_{DS(on)}$ increases and $V_{DS}$ begins to rise.
    \item The rising $V_{DS}$ charges $C_{DS}$ and discharges $C_{GD}$.
    \item The measured current includes both channel current and capacitive current components.
\end{itemize}

\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Turn-OFF Current Components}
\begin{itemize}
    \item During turn-OFF, the measured drain current is not equal to the true channel current.
    \item The channel current can be written as:
    \[
    I_{channel}
    =
    I_{DS}
    -
    C_{GD}\left|\frac{dV_{GD}}{dt}\right|
    -
    C_{DS}\frac{dV_{DS}}{dt}
    \]
    \item As $V_{DS}$ rises, $C_{GD}$ decreases, so the voltage rise may accelerate.
    \item The rapid reduction of $I_{DS}$ can generate voltage overshoot through:
    \[
    \Delta V=L_{Loop}\frac{dI_{DS}}{dt}
    \]
    \item Oscillations may appear in $V_{GS}$, $V_{DS}$, and $I_{DS}$ due to coupling through $C_{GD}$ and parasitic inductances.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Switching Energy of SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Switching energy is usually calculated from the overlap of drain current and drain-source voltage:
    \[
    E=\int v_{DS}(t)i_{DS}(t)\,dt
    \]
    \item However, measured $I_{DS}$ includes capacitive current associated with $C_{oss}$.
    \item Therefore, the channel current differs between turn-ON and turn-OFF:
    \[
    I_{channel}=I_{DS}+I_{Coss}
    \]
    \[
    I_{channel}=I_{DS}-I_{Coss}
    \]
    \item The output capacitance energy is:
    \[
    E_{oss}(V_{Bus})=
    \int_{0}^{V_{Bus}} C_{oss}(V)\cdot V_{DS}(V)\,dV
    \]
    \item Corrected switching energies can be expressed as:
    \[
    E_{on}=\int I_{DS}(t)V_{DS}(t)\,dt+E_{oss}(V_{Bus})
    \]
    \[
    E_{off}=\int I_{DS}(t)V_{DS}(t)\,dt+E_{oss}(V_{Bus})
    \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================

\begin{frame}{Gate Charge Characteristics}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Gate charge $Q_G$ represents the charge required to drive the MOSFET gate.
    \item It is obtained by integrating the gate current:
    \[
    Q_G=\int I_G(t)\,dt
    \]
    \item A constant-current gate drive is often used for measurement.
    \item $V_{GS}$ waveform can be interpreted in 4 regions:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Charging of $C_{iss}$
        \item Current rise
        \item Miller region
        \item Full enhancement
    \end{itemize}
    \item The gate charge determines gate-driver power and switching speed.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Key Design Implications}
\begin{itemize}
    \item SiC MOSFETs switch very fast, but this makes parasitic inductance and capacitance more critical.
    \item $C_{GD}$ strongly influences $dv/dt$, Miller coupling, and false turn-ON.
    \item $C_{oss}$ affects measured switching energy and must be considered in loss calculation.
    \item Reverse recovery of the body diode can still produce current overshoot and voltage ringing.
    \item Gate resistance provides a practical way to tune:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Switching speed
        \item Voltage overshoot
        \item Current overshoot
        \item EMI generation
    \end{itemize}
    \item Accurate switching-loss evaluation requires consistent definitions of integration limits.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Figure of Merit (FOM) of SiC MOSFETs}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Device selection in power electronics is not based on a single parameter.
    \item Engineers must simultaneously consider:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Conduction loss
        \item Switching loss
        \item Gate-drive loss
        \item Capacitances
        \item Thermal capability
    \end{itemize}
    \item Figure of Merit (FOM) parameters provide simplified metrics for comparing devices.
    \item A good FOM attempts to capture the tradeoff between:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Low conduction loss ($R_{DS(on)}$)
        \item Fast switching capability
        \item Low stored charge and capacitance
    \end{itemize}
    \item Different FOMs are useful for different applications such as:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Hard switching converters
        \item Soft-switching converters
        \item High-frequency operation
        \item High-voltage applications
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Baliga High-Frequency Figure of Merit (BHFFOM)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item J. Baliga proposed one of the earliest MOSFET figures of merit for high-frequency operation.
    \item The total loss is represented as the sum of:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Conduction loss due to $R_{DS(on)}$
        \item Switching loss associated with charging and discharging $C_{iss}$
    \end{itemize}

    \vspace{0.2cm}

    \[
    P_{loss}
    =
    I_{rms}^2\frac{R_{DS(on),sp}}{A}
    +
    C_{iss,sp}\cdot A \cdot V_{DRV}^2 \cdot f_s
    \]

    \item Based on this, the Baliga High-Frequency Figure of Merit is:
    \[
    \text{BHFFOM}
    =
    \frac{1}{R_{DS(on),sp}\cdot C_{iss,sp}}
    \]

    \item Since practical datasheets usually provide:
    \[
    R_{DS(on)}=\frac{R_{DS(on),sp}}{A}
    \]
    \[
    C_{iss}=C_{iss,sp}\cdot A
    \]

    the practical form becomes:
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{FOM}=R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_G
    }
    \]

    \item Lower FOM values generally indicate better switching performance.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{NHFFOM and KFOM}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Output capacitance $C_{oss}$ also contributes significantly to switching loss.
    \item Il-Jung Kim therefore proposed the Next High-Frequency Figure of Merit (NHFFOM).

    \[
    \text{NHFFOM}
    =
    \frac{1}{R_{DS(on),sp}\cdot C_{oss,sp}}
    \]

    \item Since direct use of specific capacitance is difficult, equivalent $C_{oss}$ is introduced:

    \[
    \frac{1}{2}C_{oss,eq}V^2
    =
    \int_0^V C_{oss}(V_{DS})V_{DS}\,dV_{DS}
    \]

    \item J. W. Kolar proposed the more practical KFOM: $\text{KFOM}
    =
    \frac{1}{R_{DS(on)}\cdot C_{oss,eq}}$

    \item KFOM is widely used in MHz converters because:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $C_{oss}$ strongly influences hard-switching loss
        \item Output capacitance energy becomes critical at high frequency
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{HDFOM and Idealized Switching Waveforms}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Alex Huang divided MOSFET turn-ON into several switching intervals:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Delay interval $t_d$
        \item Current rise interval $t_r$
        \item Voltage fall interval $t_f$
        \item Overdrive interval $t_{ov}$
    \end{itemize}

    \item The dominant switching loss in high-voltage devices is often associated with $t_f$.
    
    \item Based on this observation:
    \[
    P_{loss}
    =
    I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}
    +
    V\cdot I_L\cdot (t_r+t_f)f_s
    \]

    \item The resulting High-Frequency Dynamic Figure of Merit (HDFOM) becomes:

\end{itemize}

\column{0.44\textwidth}
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{HDFOM}
    =
    \sqrt{R_{DS(on)}\cdot Q_{gd}}
    }
    \]

\caption{Idealized MOSFET turn-ON switching waveform.}

\end{columns}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{YFOM for Hard-Switching Applications}
\begin{columns}[T]

\column{0.50\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Yucheng Ying proposed a more detailed switching-loss model.
    \item Both turn-ON and turn-OFF transitions are considered separately.
    \item The switching intervals are related to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Gate resistance $R_G$
        \item Miller plateau voltage $V_{plt}$
        \item Gate charge components
    \end{itemize}

    \item Important timing equations:
    \[
    t_{r(on)}
    =
    \frac{Q_{gs2}R_G}{V_{DRV}-V_{plt}}
    \]

    \[
    t_{f(on)}
    =
    \frac{Q_{gd}R_G}{V_{DRV}-V_{plt}}
    \]

\end{itemize}

\column{0.46\textwidth}

\begin{figure}
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig/lec06/YFOM_switching_process.png}
\caption{Idealized switching process for turn-ON and turn-OFF.}
\end{figure}

\end{columns}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The proposed YFOM becomes:
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{YFOM}
    =
    (Q_{gd}+K_{gs2}Q_{gs2})\cdot R_{DS(on)}
    }
    \]
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{FFOM for Soft-Switching Converters}
\begin{itemize}
    \item YFOM mainly targets hard-switching converters.
    \item Under Zero-Voltage Switching (ZVS), the turn-ON loss becomes negligible.
    \item Therefore, only these are dominant.:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Turn-OFF loss
        \item Conduction loss
        \item Gate-drive loss
    \end{itemize}


    \item The switching loss model becomes:
    \[
    P_{loss}
    =
    \frac{V_sI_s}{2}
    \frac{(Q_{gd}+Q_{gs2})R_G}{V_{plt}}
    f_s
    +
    I_{rms}^2R_{DS(on)}
    +
    Q_GV_{DRV}f_s
    \]

    \item Dianbo Fu proposed the following Figure of Merit for ZVS systems:
    \[
    \boxed{
    \text{FFOM}
    =
    (Q_{gd}+K_{loss}Q_{gs2})R_{DS(on)}
    }
    \]

    \item FFOM is particularly useful for:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item LLC resonant converters
        \item Phase-shifted full bridge converters
        \item High-frequency soft-switching applications
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Calculation of Power Device Losses}
\begin{columns}[T]
\column{0.52\textwidth}

\begin{itemize}
    \item Device loss analysis is essential for:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Thermal design
        \item Efficiency optimization
        \item Device selection
        \item Converter reliability
    \end{itemize}

    \item In practical converters, losses include:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Conduction losses
        \item Switching losses
        \item Reverse recovery losses
        \item Gate-drive losses
    \end{itemize}

    \item Loss calculations can be performed using:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Manufacturer datasheets
        \item Experimental double-pulse testing
        \item Electrothermal simulations
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\column{0.48\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Datasheet-based approaches are fast and convenient but are limited to specified operating conditions.
    
    \item Experimental measurements provide higher accuracy because:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Parasitics are included
        \item Real switching conditions are captured
        \item Reverse recovery behavior is measured directly
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Conduction Loss Calculation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Conduction loss is generated when the MOSFET carries current in the ON-state.
    \item Instantaneous conduction power is: $p(t)=v_{DS}(t)\cdot i_{DS}(t)$
    \item Average conduction loss over one switching period:
    \[
    P_C^{MOS}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \int_0^{T_s}
    V_{DS}(I_{DS}(t),T_J,V_{DRV(on)})
    \cdot
    I_{DS}(t)\,dt
    \]

    \item Since SiC MOSFET conduction is approximately resistive:
    \[
    P_C^{MOS}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \int_0^{T_s}
    R_{DS(on)}
    \cdot
    I_{DS}^2(t)\,dt
    \]

    \item Body-diode conduction loss is:
    \[
    P_C^{MOS,BD}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \int_0^{T_s}
    V_F(I_F,T_J)\cdot I_F(t)\,dt
    \]

    \item Third-quadrant conduction must include both channel conduction and body-diode conduction effects.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Switching and Reverse Recovery Losses}

\begin{columns}
\column{0.52\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Switching losses occur due to overlap between:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Drain-source voltage $V_{DS}$
        \item Drain current $I_{DS}$
    \end{itemize}

    \item Average turn-ON switching loss:
    \[
    P_{on}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \sum E_{on}
    \]

    \item Average turn-OFF switching loss:
    \[
    P_{off}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \sum E_{off}
    \]
    \end{itemize}
\column{0.52\textwidth}
    \begin{itemize}
    \item These energies are influenced by:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Junction temperature
        \item Gate resistance
        \item Loop inductance
        \item Switching current
        \item Bus voltage
    \end{itemize}

    \item Reverse recovery loss is:
    \[
    P_{rr}
    =
    \frac{1}{T_s}
    \sum E_{rr}
    \]

    \item Reverse recovery strongly depends on:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Reverse current
        \item $di/dt$
        \item Junction temperature
        \item External circuit parasitics
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{columns}  
\end{frame}
%=========================================================



%=========================================================
\begin{frame}{Total Loss Calculation}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Total MOSFET loss is the sum of all individual loss mechanisms:
    
    \[
    P_{MOS}
    =
    P_C^{MOS}
    +
    P_C^{MOS,BD}
    +
    P_C^{MOS,3rd}
    +
    P_{on}
    +
    P_{off}
    +
    P_{rr}^{MOS,BD}
    \]

    \item Total diode loss is:
    \[
    P_{Diode}
    =
    P_C^{Diode}
    +
    P_{rr}^{Diode}
    \]

    \item Device losses affect junction temperature:
    \[
    T_J \uparrow
    \Rightarrow
    R_{DS(on)} \uparrow
    \Rightarrow
    P_{loss} \uparrow
    \]

    \item Therefore, electrothermal iteration is commonly used:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Estimate losses
        \item Compute junction temperature
        \item Update temperature-dependent parameters
        \item Recalculate losses
    \end{enumerate}

    \item The process repeats until thermal steady-state convergence is achieved.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Practical Considerations in SiC Loss Analysis}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Datasheet loss values are measured under manufacturer-defined conditions.
    \item In practical converters, losses can differ significantly because of:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item PCB parasitics
        \item Gate-drive configuration
        \item Layout inductance
        \item Cooling conditions
        \item Device tolerances
    \end{itemize}

    \item Reverse recovery and switching energy are especially sensitive to:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item External loop inductance
        \item Gate resistance
        \item Commutation speed
    \end{itemize}

    \item Therefore:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Datasheets provide initial estimates
        \item Double-pulse testing validates real switching behavior
        \item Electrothermal simulation improves prediction accuracy
    \end{itemize}

    \item Accurate loss analysis is essential in:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item MHz switching converters
        \item High-power-density systems
        \item EV traction inverters
        \item Fast chargers and aerospace converters
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
